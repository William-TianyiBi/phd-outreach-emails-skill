# Source Quality and Safety Rules

Use this reference before evaluating evidence, uncertainty, funding, lab membership, or demographic information.

## Evidence Status

Label each important claim as one of:

- `Confirmed`: directly stated by a reliable source.
- `Inferred`: not directly stated, but strongly supported by multiple facts; explain the inference.
- `Unknown`: not found, stale, ambiguous, or not confidently attributable to the professor.

Do not convert an unknown into a negative claim. For example, "No active NIH grants found in searched databases" is acceptable; "the lab has no funding" is not acceptable unless a reliable source states it.

## Source Priority

Prefer sources in this order:

1. Official university, department, lab, and graduate program pages.
2. Official funding databases: NIH RePORTER, NSF Award Search, DoD/DOE/foundation award databases.
3. Publication indexes: Google Scholar, PubMed, ORCID, Semantic Scholar, DBLP, Web of Science if available.
4. Lab pages, alumni pages, institutional news, conference biographies, invited talk pages.
5. Public LinkedIn pages for professional background only.
6. Public CVs, personal websites, ORCID, Google Scholar, PubMed author pages, thesis repositories, and conference bios for student pre-admission background.
7. Third-party pages, ranking pages, student forums, and social media only as weak context; do not use them for core conclusions without stronger support.

## Citation Rules

- Cite every material factual claim with a URL and access date.
- Use direct source links in the HTML; do not hide sources behind vague labels.
- For publication and funding snapshots, include the date because counts and active awards change.
- If sources conflict, show the conflict rather than silently choosing the more convenient source.
- Avoid long quoted text. Prefer paraphrase with source links.

## Funding Evaluation

Assess funding by triangulating:

- Active/recent grants and PI/co-PI role.
- Grant end dates and recency.
- Lab size relative to visible funding.
- Institution/program baseline PhD support where applicable.
- News releases for major awards not reflected in federal databases.

Funding risk labels:

- `Low`: recent active funding and plausible support path are visible.
- `Medium`: some support signals, but gaps, stale awards, or unclear student support.
- `High`: little current evidence, expired awards, unclear appointment status, or lab size seems unsupported by visible funding.
- `Unknown`: databases/pages are insufficient; use this when evidence is missing rather than weak.

## Lab Composition and Demographics

Do not infer race, ethnicity, caste, religion, or nationality from names, photos, languages, accent, appearance, or institutions.

Record only:

- Public self-identification by the person.
- Official aggregate diversity information from the lab, department, or program.
- Academic and professional background: role, degree institution, prior institution, training area, methods, career stage, public alumni outcome.
- International training signals when directly stated, such as an institution location or explicit biography, without converting that into citizenship or identity claims.

If the user asks for race or ethnicity, state that the dossier will not infer sensitive identity. If there is no explicit self-identification or aggregate official source, write `Not assessed; no public self-identification or official aggregate data found`.

## PhD Student Pre-Admission Background

Use student background analysis to estimate the lab's observable admissions baseline. Do not judge students or imply that unpublished/private history does not exist.

For each current or recent PhD student, collect only public information:

- Prior institution(s) and degree(s), when stated.
- Approximate PhD start or lab-join date, when stated or inferable from lab news/thesis timeline.
- Pre-PhD publications, counted only when publication date and authorship plausibly precede PhD start/lab join.
- Publication signal for each confirmed/possible paper: title, year, journal, student author position, first/co-first/corresponding status if public, journal impact factor or quartile/rank when sourced, and metric source/year.
- Prior research roles: undergraduate thesis, master's research, research assistant, postbacc, technician, industry research, clinical research, internships, or fellowships.
- Public methods/domain background relevant to the PI's lab.

LinkedIn use rules:

- Use only public LinkedIn content visible without login or provided in search snippets/browser-accessible pages.
- Treat LinkedIn as strong evidence for education, degree timing, employer, research assistant/technician roles, internships, and broad skill areas when the profile identity is clear.
- Confirm identity by matching at least two of: current university/program, PI/lab, field, location, coauthor network, prior institution, or profile links.
- Do not scrape private content, infer sensitive identity, or use profile photos for any conclusion.
- If LinkedIn is missing, private, stale, or ambiguous, mark the relevant field `Unknown`.

Google Scholar and publication-profile rules:

- Use Google Scholar/ORCID/PubMed/Semantic Scholar to identify publications, not personal demographics.
- Attribute a profile or paper to the student only when at least one disambiguating signal matches: institution, PI/lab coauthors, topic, email/domain, ORCID, lab publication list, or thesis/CV cross-reference.
- For common names, require stronger confirmation. Same name alone is insufficient.
- If affiliation reflects the PhD institution, do not assume the paper predates PhD entry unless the date and timeline support it.

Publication-count rules:

- `Confirmed pre-PhD publication`: the paper date is before the student's PhD start/lab join date, or a public CV clearly places it before PhD entry.
- `Possible pre-PhD publication`: authorship is public, but start date is unclear; report separately from confirmed counts.
- `Unknown`: no reliable profile, start date, or publication record is found.
- Never write "0 publications" unless both the publication search and timeline evidence are strong enough to support it; prefer "no public pre-PhD publications found."

Author-order and journal-metric rules:

- Determine author position from PubMed, publisher pages, DOI pages, ORCID records, or lab publication lists. Count positions precisely when possible: first, co-first, middle, second, senior/last, corresponding.
- Equal contribution and corresponding authorship require explicit evidence from the article page, PDF, PubMed contribution notes, publisher metadata, or a public CV. Do not infer equal contribution from name order.
- Journal impact factor and quartile/ranking are time-sensitive. Record the metric year and source, such as "2024 JIF", "JCR category quartile", or "Scimago SJR quartile".
- Prefer official JCR/Clarivate or journal pages. If using third-party metric sites because official JCR is inaccessible, label the metric `approximate` and cite the source.
- Do not compare papers across fields using impact factor alone. Use IF/quartile as a rough signal alongside author position, paper type, field, and relevance to the PI's lab.
- If no reliable metric is found quickly, write `Metric not confirmed` rather than guessing.

Aggregate cautiously:

- Use ranges and visibility language, such as "among 4 visible PhD profiles, 2 had confirmed prior publications and 2 were unknown."
- Separate "prior institution" from prestige judgments. Do not imply school rank alone caused admission.
- Do not infer nationality, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, citizenship, or visa status from names, institutions, photos, or LinkedIn location.

## Recommendation Integrity

Separate opportunity quality from personal fit unless the user supplies their background. Professor-only recommendation should weigh:

- Active research trajectory.
- Funding/support visibility.
- Evidence of current student mentoring.
- Observable backgrounds of current/recent PhD students, especially whether prior publications or substantial research employment appear common.
- Program pathway compatibility for PhD applicants.
- Recency and reliability of public information.
- Outreach usefulness: whether an email can ask a concrete, answerable question.

Always include a confidence level. Low confidence is not a criticism of the professor; it means the public record is thin or ambiguous.
